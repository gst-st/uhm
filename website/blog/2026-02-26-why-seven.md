A detector says that one of several wires has failed. It must tell us which wire, and it must distinguish every failure from a healthy machine. How many positions are needed? The answer depends on what the detector can do.

<!-- truncate -->

Perfect correction of a single binary error with more than two codewords gives a lower bound of seven. It cannot decide the question alone: a fifteen-position Hamming code works as well. An independent experiment restricts the address alphabet from the other side. It compares two interference settings on every specified rectangle of a signed circuit. If both tests have the required visibility and the same address decodes every error position plus the no-error outcome, the two bounds meet at seven positions.

The assumption about the *same* address matters. Two unrelated devices can each pass their own test without imposing this shared bound. An actual apparatus must implement both operations, with the same address and a fixed decoder. Seven positions in such a device do not imply that all matter or every mind has seven coordinates.
