Count the waves on a ring of seven positions. One pattern is constant. The other six patterns come in opposite-frequency pairs: three real oscillation planes. Nothing in this count mentions the electron, the muon or the tau. Changing the ring to nine positions would give four planes.

<!-- truncate -->

To identify a plane with a family of particles we would need a device that couples to its transformations in the observed way. Then we could calculate something the count does not already contain: for instance, a detector rate at energies that were not used to choose those couplings. The three-plane count is exact. The family assignment is a physical hypothesis with a separate test.

The distinction matters experimentally. The visible shape of the Z resonance and its invisible width constrain light species with specified couplings. Accelerator experiments measure rates, not Fourier pairs.
